Transcript Quality Is an Audio Problem, Not an AI Problem

Speech-to-text is guesswork with very good odds. Given a clear signal, modern models guess brilliantly. Given a laptop fan, a cafe and two people talking over each other, they guess badly and confidently, producing tidy sentences nobody said. That is why swapping one automatic meeting note taker for another rarely rescues a bad transcript. The tools all sit downstream of the same broken audio. Clean the signal and every option you audition improves at once, for free.

  • Constant noise from fans, AC or traffic, which smears consonants and invents words.
  • Cross-talk, where two speakers overlap and the speaker labels fall apart.
  • A laptop mic sitting under your hands, catching every keystroke.
  • Heavy compression on a weak connection, dropping syllables before the AI ever hears them.

That pairing is why an AI meeting note taker attached to noise cancellation makes sense rather than being a bundle gimmick. Krisp is the plain example: it strips background noise on the way into your call and ships an AI note taker and transcription feature alongside, with audio processed on your own device rather than shipped to a server for cleanup. One layer, clean input and notes out the other side. Whatever you settle on, judge it on transcript quality in the room you actually work in, not on a demo recorded in a padded studio by someone with a broadcast microphone.

The limit that bites is rarely the headline one

Minutes get the headline. Retention and export are what actually stop you. A free tier that holds transcripts for a short window, or lets you read notes in a browser but never export them as text, is a trial wearing a disguise. Retention windows also shrink quietly, so export anything you care about the same week you record it. Check the export formats before you rely on any of this.

How to Judge a Note Taker App in Ten Minutes

Run one test call through every candidate and score five things. Free minutes: how many, and do they reset daily, monthly or never? Storage: are recordings and transcripts held on the vendor's servers, for how long, and can you delete them on demand? Capture method: does it join as a visible bot participant, or record locally from your machine? Bots are easy to set up and impossible to hide; local capture is quieter but ties you to one device. Exports: plain text, Markdown, subtitles, or nothing at all. Platforms: Zoom, Teams, Google Meet, and whether in-person recording is supported at all.

Then test in your worst room, not your best one. Record five minutes with the fan running, read the transcript, count the errors. Run it again with a cleanup layer in front and compare the two. That gap tells you where your effort belongs. If the second pass is dramatically better, you have an audio problem, and no amount of upgrading will fix it. Repeat the test on each platform you actually use, because coverage and accuracy are not the same everywhere. Edge Cases: In-Person, Teams, and the Bot in the Room

In-person meetings

Recording a room is a different problem from recording a call. One laptop mic in the middle of a table captures the two people nearest it and mangles everyone else. Slide the device toward whoever is quietest. Live noise cancellation is desktop software: Krisp's mobile apps handle transcription and meeting notes, not live noise removal (checked August 2026).

Microsoft Teams, Zoom and Meet

Platform coverage is uneven and changes often. Some tools join through your calendar automatically; others need starting by hand. If you live in Microsoft Teams or Zoom, verify the integration exists on the free tier specifically, because integrations are a classic paid-only feature. On a machine with an NVIDIA RTX 2060 or better, NVIDIA Broadcast cleans the mic feed for free whatever platform you use.

Consent, and who reads it later

Recording law varies by state and country, and "the AI did it" is not a defense. Announce recording at the top of the call, every time. Then ask where the transcript lives, because anything on a vendor's server is readable by that vendor and discoverable later. On-device processing skips the upload entirely.

Meeting Notes FAQ

What does an AI meeting note taker actually do?

It captures your call audio, transcribes it, and generates a structured summary: key points, decisions and action items. Some tools join the call as a bot participant, others record locally from your device. The transcript is the raw material and the summary is the product.

Is there a genuinely free option?

Yes, but always metered. Free plans cap monthly minutes, meeting counts, retention windows or exports. Krisp's free plan includes 60 minutes a day of noise cancellation on a single device with no credit card, plus its note taker and transcription feature (checked August 2026).

Does it work for in-person meetings?

It can, with worse results. Room audio means distance, echo and overlapping voices, all of which hurt accuracy and speaker labeling. Put the recording device near the quietest person, cut hard surfaces where you can, and expect to edit names and jargon afterward. Live noise cancellation is desktop-only, so a laptop beats a phone.

Can it take notes in Microsoft Teams?

Many tools support Teams, but support on the free tier is a separate question from support in general. Check three things: whether it joins automatically via your calendar, whether your admin allows external bots into calls, and whether transcripts export. Any one of those can quietly block the workflow you planned.

Why is my transcript full of errors?

Almost always the audio. Background noise, cross-talk, a distant mic and lossy compression push the model into low-confidence guesses that read as fluent nonsense. Clean the input first: closer mic, quieter room, noise cancellation in front of the call. That single change beats switching tools nearly every time.
